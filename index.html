1. Overview

1.1 Why the choice of method matters

Teaching English to young learners is a process of developing language competence through exposure, interaction, practice, and meaningful use of English. It is not only about memorising vocabulary and grammar structures, but also about helping children develop listening, speaking, reading, and writing appropriate to their age.

The choice of method should be based on several factors: age, language proficiency, learning objectives, cognitive characteristics, classroom conditions, and teaching resources. No single method is best for every child or every educational situation. Teachers must select and combine methods flexibly, with professional justification, and in line with lesson objectives.

Three main benefits
  • Enhancing learning effectiveness — appropriate content and activities help children understand, retain, and apply language knowledge better.
  • Increasing learning motivation — meaningful, achievable, and interactive activities help children stay engaged and reduce pressure when using the new language.
  • Developing long-term language competence — combining exposure, guided practice, and authentic communication creates conditions for balanced skill development.

1.2 Basic principles

  • Appropriate to level — tasks should be suitably challenging with appropriate support.
  • Enhance interaction — children need opportunities to listen, speak, and use English in situations with clear purposes.
  • Combine practice and communication — vocabulary, pronunciation, and grammar practice should support language use rather than become the sole goal.
  • Regular assessment — teachers should observe, check, and adjust activities based on children's actual progress.
  • Flexible organisation — images, audio, movement, and games should serve learning objectives, not just entertain.
Caution about learning styles

Children's learning preferences vary, but there is insufficient evidence to claim that categorising children into fixed groups such as "visual learners", "auditory learners", or "kinesthetic learners" and teaching each group separately improves learning outcomes. Instead, teachers should choose presentation formats appropriate to the content and combine supports when needed (Pashler et al., 2008).

1.3 Key terminology to know

  • Method — an overall plan for presenting language material, based on a theory of language and language learning (e.g., Grammar-Translation, Audio-Lingual).
  • Approach — a set of assumptions about language and learning (e.g., TBLT, TPR).
  • Technique — a specific classroom activity or procedure (e.g., a substitution drill, a picture-matching game).
  • Strategy — a learner's own plan for learning (e.g., Spaced Repetition, Retrieval Practice).

2. Traditional Methods

In this document, traditional describes approaches typically associated with explanation of knowledge, controlled practice, and pattern-based practice. This grouping serves learning purposes and does not mean these methods are obsolete or lack value.

2.1 Grammar-Translation Method

Concept & features

Focuses on teaching grammar rules, vocabulary, and translating texts between the mother tongue and the target language. Teachers explain, give examples, and have students apply rules through exercises such as verb conjugation, sentence transformation, translation, and reading comprehension. The method typically uses L1 to explain difficult points. Reading and writing are prominent; spontaneous communication may not be emphasised.

Advantages
  • Helps students recognise and analyse grammatical structures systematically.
  • Supports vocabulary, reading comprehension, and text translation.
  • Suitable for tasks requiring grammatical accuracy and language analysis.
  • Easy to organise in resource-limited classrooms (no technology required).
Limitations
  • If used as the main method, may limit opportunities to practise listening and speaking.
  • Memorising rules and translating sentences does not automatically ensure communicative use.
  • Repetitive, decontextualised activities may reduce children's engagement.
  • Heavy reliance on translation may hinder processing information directly in English.
Classroom example (children)

When teaching the present simple for habits, the teacher displays a short illustrated text about a character's day, explains the rule in Vietnamese, and asks children to complete sentence-transformation exercises. To keep it age-appropriate, pair the explanation with a picture-matching activity and one short oral question round.

Teacher tips

Use GTM selectively for specific grammar points or reading tasks. Supplement with visual aids and short communicative activities. Avoid long grammar lectures for young children.

2.2 Audio-Lingual Method

Concept & features

Emerged strongly in the 20th century, influenced by behaviourism and structural views of language. Emphasises listening to language models, imitation, pattern practice, and reinforcement of appropriate responses.

Typical activities:

  • Repeating words, phrases, and model sentences.
  • Pattern-dialogue practice.
  • Substitution drills (replacing one element in a sentence).
  • Transformation drills (e.g., affirmative → negative → question).
  • Pronunciation and intonation practice through listening and imitation.
Advantages
  • Provides opportunities to practise pronunciation, stress, intonation, and common sentence patterns.
  • Helps beginners become familiar with language structures through guided practice.
  • Supports initial fluency with frequently practised patterns.
  • Can be organised with the teacher modelling directly; recording equipment is a support, not a requirement.
Limitations
  • Mechanical practice may lead to memorisation without deep understanding of usage.
  • Excessive focus on accuracy may reduce experimentation and personal expression.
  • Does not automatically ensure balanced development of all four skills.
  • Repetition must be supplemented with meaningful communication.
Classroom example (children)

Teacher: "I like apples." → Students repeat. Teacher substitutes: "bananas" → Students: "I like bananas." Continue with "I don't like…" using a thumbs-down gesture. Finish with a 2-minute "chain game" where each child adds one item.

Teacher tips

Keep drills short (2–3 minutes). Connect patterns to a clear context. Follow up with a quick communicative activity so students use the pattern purposefully.

3. Modern Methods & Strategies

3.1 Task-Based Language Teaching (TBLT)

Concept & features

An approach in which learners use language to complete a task with a specific goal and outcome. Instead of isolated structure practice, students use English to exchange information, solve problems, or create a product.

Three features of a task:

  • Focus on meaning and communicative goals.
  • Requires learners to use their language resources to complete the task.
  • Has an identifiable outcome or product (a plan, decision, or presentation).

TBLT has many implementation variants. It does not require teachers to eliminate grammar or vocabulary teaching entirely; form-focused activities can be combined when needed (Ellis, 2009).

Classroom example

Groups plan a one-day healthy menu, choose suitable foods, discuss choices, and present their menu in English.

Advantages
  • Creates opportunities to use English in purpose-driven situations.
  • Encourages information exchange, cooperation, and problem-solving.
  • Can combine listening, speaking, reading, and writing depending on the task.
  • Allows teachers to observe language application during task completion.
Limitations
  • Task design requires clear objectives, language support, and assessment criteria.
  • Low-proficiency students may struggle if tasks exceed their current level.
  • Some students may participate less if roles are not well assigned.
  • Assessment must consider both outcome and language use, not only product completion.
Teacher tips

Design short tasks (5–10 minutes). Provide sentence frames and key vocabulary. Assign roles. Monitor and support all groups. Debrief language use after the task.

3.2 Gamification

Concept & features

Using game elements — points, challenges, levels, badges, immediate feedback — in a non-game activity to support engagement and motivation.

In English teaching, gamification can be implemented through classroom activities such as vocabulary challenges, team missions, sentence-matching games, or online quiz apps.

Distinguish from Game-Based Learning (GBL): in gamification, game elements are integrated into learning activities; in GBL, the game itself is the main vehicle for organising the learning experience.

Advantages
  • Increases interaction and feedback opportunities.
  • Can help students maintain attention and engagement.
  • Creates practice opportunities for vocabulary, grammar, and patterns.
  • Allows quick comprehension checks through student responses.
Limitations
  • Students may focus on points or rewards more than on learning objectives.
  • Public competition may pressure some children, especially speed-only activities.
  • Games disconnected from objectives may entertain without producing learning.
  • Some online activities require devices and internet; however, gamification can also be implemented with paper cards, whiteboards, and group activities.
Teacher tips

Align games with language objectives. Avoid elimination games. Use cooperative formats. Always debrief after the game so play connects to learning.

3.3 Total Physical Response (TPR)

Concept & features

Developed by James J. Asher, TPR emphasises the connection between listening comprehension and physical responses. In the early stages, the teacher gives commands in English and students perform the corresponding actions.

Examples:

  • "Stand up." — Students stand up.
  • "Touch your head." — Students touch their head.
  • "Walk to the door." — Students walk to the door.

After comprehension opportunities, the teacher can encourage students to give commands themselves or use similar structures in communication.

TPR was presented by Asher (1969) as an approach to second-language learning through the coordination of speech and action.

Advantages
  • Supports understanding and retention of vocabulary, especially action verbs, body parts, and instructions.
  • Allows physical response before verbal expression.
  • Suitable for visual, interactive listening comprehension activities.
  • Can reduce speaking pressure for beginners.
Limitations
  • Insufficient on its own for comprehensive language development.
  • Less suitable for abstract content or complex reasoning tasks.
  • Physical activities need appropriate space, class size, and safety arrangements.
  • Repetition without expansion may become monotonous.
Teacher tips

Keep sessions short (5–10 minutes). Combine with songs. Gradually increase complexity. Use humour. Later, let students give the commands.

3.4 Spaced Repetition

Concept & features

A learning strategy in which learners review knowledge multiple times over spaced intervals rather than massing study in one session. It can be combined with retrieval practice — actively trying to recall information without immediately looking at the answer.

Example: after learning new words, students review by recalling meanings, naming objects in English, or using words in sentences in later sessions.

Intervals need not be fixed; they can be adjusted based on content difficulty, retention level, and review results. Carpenter, Pan & Butler (2022) show that spacing and retrieval practice are research-supported learning strategies. However, the specific effect depends on content, timing, and assessment method.

Advantages
  • Supports long-term knowledge retention.
  • Encourages active recall instead of passive rereading.
  • Can reveal words or structures students have not yet mastered.
  • Easy to combine with flashcards, short questions, and on-class or at-home review.
Limitations
  • Requires a consistent and reasonable review schedule.
  • Reviewing isolated words may lead to knowing meanings but not usage.
  • Flashcard systems must be accurately designed with context and level-appropriate content.
  • Technology can support organisation but is not a requirement.
Teacher tips

Build short 5-minute review slots into each lesson. Use flashcards. Connect review to usage (e.g., "Use last week's word in a sentence").

5. Selection Guide

The age groups below are directional only. Teachers should consider each child's proficiency, cognitive ability, learning experience, and actual needs.

Preschool (ages ~3–5)

Prioritise TPR, songs, picture books, games, and short listening activities. Use simple language, model directly, and create opportunities for children to respond physically before requiring verbal expression.

Early primary (ages ~6–8)

Combine TPR, games, pattern practice, and short communicative activities. Begin establishing regular spaced-review habits. Develop reading and writing at an appropriate level.

Late primary (ages ~9–12)

Increase communicative tasks, group projects, reading, writing, and discussion. Combine selective grammar explanation, pattern practice, and spaced repetition to consolidate knowledge.

Combining methods by lesson objective

  • Introduce new vocabulary → TPR + flashcards + game.
  • Practise structures → short Audio-Lingual drills + a task.
  • Develop speaking → TBLT + role-play.
  • Consolidate → spaced review + quiz game.
  • Explain grammar to older learners → short grammar explanation + application task.

6. Sample 45-minute Lesson — Healthy Food

Step 1 (5 min) — Warm-up with TPR

Objective: Activate prior knowledge; review food vocabulary.

Teacher: Give commands — "Touch the apple", "Point to the banana", "Stand up if you like rice."

Students: Listen and perform actions.

English examples: "Touch the apple.", "Point to the banana.", "Show me a healthy food."

Check: Observe correct physical responses.

Step 2 (10 min) — Introduce language and guided practice

Objective: Introduce target vocabulary and structures.

Teacher: Present vocabulary (apple, banana, rice, vegetables, milk) with visuals. Model "I like…", "I don't like…", "My favourite food is…".

Students: Listen, repeat, complete sentence frames.

English examples: "I like apples.", "I don't like soda.", "My favourite food is rice."

Check: Monitor pair practice; correct errors gently.

Step 3 (15 min) — Communicative task (TBLT)

Objective: Use language to complete a meaningful task.

Teacher: Assign group task — "Plan a healthy lunch menu." Provide sentence frames and key vocabulary. Circulate and support.

Students: Work in groups: discuss, choose foods, prepare presentation.

English examples: "Let's have rice and vegetables.", "Do you like fish?", "Our menu has…"

Check: Observe collaboration and language use; check each group's menu.

Step 4 (10 min) — Consolidation with Gamification

Objective: Reinforce vocabulary and structures.

Teacher: Run "Healthy Food Bingo" or "Picture Match" in teams. Award points for correct answers.

Students: Participate in teams; respond quickly.

English examples: "Bingo! Apple!", "Is this healthy? Yes!"

Check: Note which items students miss for future review.

Step 5 (5 min) — Assessment and homework

Objective: Consolidate learning; assign spaced review.

Teacher: Ask each student to say one new word and one sentence about their favourite food. Assign short review for the next sessions.

Students: Say or write words and sentence.

English examples: "Apple. My favourite food is apple."

Check: Quick oral check; collect a 1-sentence exit ticket.

Method integration

TPR supports comprehension; guided practice supports form; TBLT creates purposeful use; gamification consolidates; spaced repetition maintains knowledge after the lesson.
